## Purpose

Capture observations, reference analysis, and patterns during the research phase. This is a working document — write freely, synthesize periodically.

**Note on methodology:** Batch 1 analyses are based on training knowledge (May 2025), not live site fetches. Core positioning and structural approaches are well-established for these studios and unlikely to have changed dramatically, but specific copy or layout details may have shifted.

## References — Batch 1: Direct Landscape

These are studios operating in SMASH's competitive territory: immersive, interactive, spatial experiences.

---

### 1. Moment Factory
**momentfactory.com** | ~300 employees, Montreal HQ, global offices | Reviewed 2026-04-01

**5-second test:** Big. Cinematic. Spectacle. Full-bleed video of large-scale installations. You know they do "immersive experiences" but the first impression is awe, not understanding.

**30-second test:** "Multimedia entertainment studio that creates large-scale immersive experiences — projection mapping, interactive installations, concert visuals, permanent attractions." Clarity arrives through the work, not the copy.

**Positioning:** Generic. "Creating environments that engage and inspire" could describe a thousand studios. Their specificity comes from scale and client roster (Changi Airport, Super Bowl), not from language.

**Tone:** Corporate-cinematic. Reads like an annual report written by someone who watches Netflix trailers. Professional, impersonal, heavy on superlatives. Competent but never memorable.

**Project storytelling:** Challenge-vision-result arc. Functional but formulaic. Describes what happened more than what it felt like or meant. No deep technical breakdowns, minimal credits, no honest discussion of constraints.

**Trust signals:** Client roster (marquee names everywhere), global offices, longevity (20+ years), volume (hundreds of projects), professional media quality throughout.

**What works:** The work genuinely leads. Clear taxonomy for filtering projects. Consistent case study format creates scannability. Professional media quality.

**What doesn't work:** Zero personality — you can't distinguish who these people are. Generic "immersive experiences" language. Scale as entire identity. No process transparency. Case studies lack depth.

**For SMASH:** Don't build a smaller version of this. Build the opposite. Dense, fast, personal, opinionated, technically transparent. MF structurally can't be personal or show process. That's open territory.

### 2. teamLab
**teamlab.art** | ~500+ members, Tokyo | Reviewed 2026-04-01

**5-second test:** Awe. Spectacle. Scale. Reads as an institution, not a studio. Full-bleed glowing organic environments — flowers, water, light. Closer to a museum website than a services company.

**30-second test:** "Large interdisciplinary collective that creates immersive digital art environments — permanent museums, traveling exhibitions, standalone artworks." Positioned as artists, not a studio for hire.

**Positioning:** "Art collective investigating the relationship between the self and the world." Specific in aesthetic (instantly recognizable visual signature), generic in language (art-world abstraction). The work is the brand — no positioning statement needed.

**Tone:** Poetic-philosophical with Japanese sensibility. Contemplative. Unhurried. Zero sales language, zero urgency. Reads like an artist monograph, not a business website.

**Project storytelling:** Concept-first. Describes the idea and the experience, never the tech stack. Written like exhibition wall text. Technology is deliberately invisible — you'll rarely see "100 projectors" mentioned.

**Trust signals:** Sheer volume and scale of permanent museums worldwide. Cultural recognition. Visitor numbers (Borderless was one of the most visited single-artist museums globally). Visual consistency across all work.

**What works:** Work leads with zero preamble. Visual consistency builds brand faster than copy. The website is a tool, not a performance — surprisingly restrained UI. Concept-first storytelling is powerful.

**What doesn't work:** No contact pathway (luxury SMASH can't afford). Anonymous collective model hides humans. Single aesthetic trap — every project looks like a teamLab project. Art-world register excludes commercial clients.

**For SMASH:** teamLab's site works because they're selling tickets to the public. SMASH needs to convince someone to pick up the phone. That requires clarity about capabilities, named humans who inspire trust, and zero friction to "let's talk." The aesthetic can be confident and dark, but the communication strategy needs to be direct where teamLab is oblique, specific where they're philosophical, and human where they're institutional.

### 3. Random International
**random-international.com** | London-based art practice, small team | Reviewed 2026-04-01

**5-second test:** Art practice, not commercial studio. Large atmospheric imagery. Gallery-white, restrained, European-intellectual. "The Rain Room people." Prestigious, cool (in the temperature sense). Deliberate distance.

**30-second test:** "Art studio that makes large-scale installations exploring the relationship between humans and technology." What's harder to grasp: how you'd actually work with them. The process is a black box by design.

**Positioning:** Fine art practice, not services. "Investigating" / "exploring" / "questioning." Technology framed as medium, never selling point. Specific within the art world, potentially vague outside it.

**Tone:** Clinical-poetic. Sparse, measured, European art-world editorial. Short declarative sentences. Gallery wall text energy. Creates distance intentionally — signals serious practitioners, not approachable collaborators.

**Project storytelling:** Hero image → poetic description (1-3 sentences) → venue credits (MoMA, Barbican — the venues do the trust work) → additional media. Narrative centers on the experience of encountering the work, not the making of it. Strong but deliberately opaque about process.

**Trust signals:** Venue names (MoMA, Barbican, LACMA validate everything). Rain Room cultural recognition. Photography quality. Press coverage. Longevity (founded 2005).

**What works:** Restraint reads as confidence. Minimal UI stays out of the way. They don't oversell. The work carries it.

**What doesn't work:** Zero entry point for collaboration. Black-box process. Clinical tone excludes anyone outside the art world. If you're a festival or brand looking to commission work, you have no idea how to engage.

**For SMASH:** RI succeeds by being inaccessible on purpose — it's a gatekeeping mechanism that signals art-world belonging. SMASH needs the opposite: **accessible confidence**. The work is serious, the craft is deep, but the door is open. Show the people, show the process, be warm where RI is cold, make contact frictionless.

### 4. Studio Roosegaarde
**studioroosegaarde.net** | ~20-30 people, Rotterdam | Reviewed 2026-04-01

**5-second test:** One person's vision. Landscape-scale installation with people in it. "Roosegaarde" dominates. Closer to an architect's portfolio than a creative agency. Impressive, curated, deliberate. Not warm or inviting — impressive.

**30-second test:** "Large-scale light installations and public design projects about climate, energy, and landscape. One guy's vision with a team in Rotterdam." You would NOT say "they're a studio you can hire for your event." Auteur, not agency.

**Positioning:** "Social design lab." Artist and innovator. "Landscapes of the future." Specific in domain (public space, light, environment), generic in language ("innovation" appears too much). Saved by the work being distinctive enough that generic language doesn't dilute it.

**Tone:** Poetic-editorial with Dutch directness. Short sentences. Provocative questions. Present tense. Between museum wall text and TED talk script. "What if the beauty of the northern lights could show the power of water?"

**Project storytelling:** This is their genuine strength. Structure: provocation (the problem/question) → concept (the idea, designed to be quotable) → experience (sensory language, what audiences feel) → impact (actual metrics — smog collected, energy saved) → technical details (present but subordinate). Starting with "why it matters" instead of "what we built" makes every project feel urgent.

**Trust signals:** Scale photography with humans. Press logos (Forbes, Dezeen, Guardian). Awards. Institutional partners (UNESCO, Dutch government). Impact metrics. Daan himself (TED talks, books, public profile). Longevity (10+ years of projects).

**What works:** Provocation-first storytelling is powerful. Photography with humans communicates scale instantly. Impact metrics make poetic claims tangible. Dark restrained UI lets work be the color. Consistency — portfolio reads as a body of work, not a list of jobs.

**What doesn't work:** The Daan show — if he leaves, the brand collapses. No entry point for collaboration. Inaccessible positioning (mid-sized brand wanting an installation gets no signal they'd take the call). Slow site performance. Thin on technical depth.

**For SMASH:** Steal the storytelling discipline (provocation-first, impact metrics, sensory experience language). Skip the auteur model — SMASH is three equal partners, and that collaborative identity is a differentiator. Be specific about capabilities without being a boring service list. Show range quickly rather than single-project hero dominance. Be more direct, more grounded, more Swedish.

### 5. &Walsh
**andwalsh.com** | NYC, founded by Jessica Walsh | Reviewed 2026-04-01

**5-second test:** Design studio led by a singular creative personality. Large-scale, vivid, color-saturated project imagery. You know within seconds this is branding and visual identity at a high level.

**30-second test:** "Brand design and art direction studio in New York. Visual identity, branding, packaging, campaigns. Bold, maximalist, color-driven." Clarity is high — the work speaks.

**Positioning:** Personal-brand-as-studio. Portfolio IS the positioning — what you show and omit says more than any services page. Medium-high specificity through curation rather than description.

**Tone:** Confident-casual. Not corporate, not poetic. Reads like a smart creative professional explaining their work to a peer. Declarative. Short sentences. No hype. No superlatives. The work is presented as fact, not pitch.

**Project storytelling:** Excellent — and the most transferable model so far. Structure: hero image (the single most striking visual) → brief/challenge (strategic framing, not task description) → visual narrative (carefully sequenced, rhythmic: wide, close, wide, detail) → outcome. Each case study feels art-directed as content in itself, not a dump of deliverables.

**Trust signals:** Client roster embedded in projects (not a logo grid). Press/awards as metadata, not headlines. Volume and consistency signal operational maturity. Jessica's personal brand pre-qualifies the studio.

**For SMASH:** The key lesson is **treat case studies as creative projects**. Sequence images with rhythm. Frame challenges in human/strategic terms, not deliverables. "The festival needed its audience to feel X before they even entered the building" beats "we built a projection mapping installation." Also: SMASH has three voices where &Walsh has one — that's a structural differentiator. And SMASH should show technical process where &Walsh doesn't need to.

### 6. Pentagram
**pentagram.com** | Multi-city, 25+ partners | Reviewed 2026-04-01

**5-second test:** Authority. Restraint. The homepage IS the work — a full-bleed scrolling grid of project imagery. Almost no copy. The absence of a tagline is itself a positioning choice: "you already know who we are."

**30-second test:** "Major design firm — identity, branding, environmental, digital. Everything visual at a high level." You get this from scanning the grid, not from any statement.

**Positioning:** Structural, not ideological. Differentiation is *how they're organized* (independent, partner-led, equal ownership) rather than *what they believe about design*. The work carries the ideology.

**Tone:** Editorial-institutional. Third person. Declarative. Newspaper register. "Pentagram has designed..." not "We designed..." Removes sales energy entirely. You never feel marketed to.

**Project storytelling:** The gold standard for minimal framing. Structure: hero image → title + client + partner name → 2-4 sentence framing paragraph → 15-30 carefully art-directed images → credits. No challenge/solution/result. No process. No metrics. The work is a fait accompli — here it is.

**Trust signals:** Client logos by implication (embedded in projects, never a grid). Volume over decades. Partner pedigree. The homepage itself — an uninterrupted wall of quality — IS the trust signal.

**For SMASH:** Adopt the philosophy (work-first, no fluff), not the temperature (cold, institutional). Key steals: credit team roles on every project (Creative Direction: Ash / Production: Veronica / Tech: Emile). No testimonials, no logo grids — let client names live inside project cards. Kill decorative interaction. But add what Pentagram omits: process, tech, warmth, and a positioning line for visitors who don't already know you.

**snohetta.com** | 300+ people, Oslo/NYC/global | Reviewed 2026-04-01

## Competitive landscape

### Where SMASH sits

All four direct-landscape studios share a pattern: **the work leads, the process is hidden, commercial engagement is obscured.**

This creates a gap in the market:

| What the landscape does | What's missing |
|------------------------|----------------|
| Spectacle-first presentation | Accessible entry point for collaboration |
| Art-world or corporate tone | Warm, direct, practitioner voice |
| Process as black box | Technical transparency and craft pride |
| Anonymous or auteur identity | Named humans you'd want in the room |
| Generic "immersive experiences" language | Specific, concrete descriptions of what they do |
| Scale as primary trust signal | Craft, intent, and partnership as trust signals |

SMASH can't compete on scale, venue names, or global recognition. But none of these studios offer: **accessible confidence, technical transparency, named collaborative partners, or specific-yet-poetic language.** That territory is open.

## Patterns emerging (updated after 8 references)

### Things that keep working (8/8 references confirm)
- **Work leads.** Every strong studio puts imagery before explanation. Zero "welcome to our studio" preamble. Pentagram is the purest example — the homepage IS the grid.
- **Photography/video quality is non-negotiable.** The documentation IS the portfolio. Bad photos undercut everything. For immersive work specifically, video is essential — stills of projections can look flat (Snøhetta insight).
- **Restraint in UI reads as confidence.** The best sites are frames for the work, not performances. Even &Walsh — whose client work is exuberant — keeps their own site restrained. The site is the frame, not the painting.
- **Concept-first or provocation-first storytelling** beats challenge/solution/result every time. Roosegaarde (provocation), teamLab (concept), &Walsh (strategic framing) all outperform Moment Factory's formulaic arc.
- **Case studies as editorial content.** The strongest project pages (&Walsh, Pentagram, Snøhetta) are designed with the same care as the work itself — image sequencing, rhythm, pacing.

### Things that keep failing (consistent across batches)
- **Generic positioning language.** "Immersive experiences," "we create experiences," "engaging environments" — interchangeable across every studio. The words do no work.
- **Corporate tone** (Moment Factory) — professional but personality-free.
- **Art-world tone** (Random International, teamLab, Universal Everything) — prestigious but gates out commercial clients.
- **Hiding the people.** Anonymous collectives, auteur models, institutional voice — none build the personal trust a commissioner needs.
- **No clear pathway to collaboration.** 7 of 8 references make it difficult to understand how to actually work with them.
- **Process as black box.** Universal across the landscape. Impressive but doesn't build the trust a commercial client needs before spending money.

### What no one does well (the open territory)
- Show the humans behind the work as approachable, named collaborators
- Explain process/craft with pride rather than hiding it
- Use a warm, direct, practitioner voice (&Walsh comes closest but it's still one-voice, not collaborative)
- Make it genuinely easy and inviting to start a conversation
- Be technically specific without becoming a spec sheet
- Credit team roles on every project (Pentagram credits partners — SMASH should credit all three roles)
- Combine editorial confidence with commercial clarity

## Observations by topic (updated after 8 references)

### Project storytelling
- **Provocation-first is strongest:** Roosegaarde's approach (start with why it matters → concept → experience → impact) is the most compelling narrative structure across all 8 references.
- **Editorial case studies work:** &Walsh and Pentagram prove that treating each project page as designed content (sequenced images, rhythm, pacing) creates more impact than templated dumps.
- **Metadata-first orientation:** Snøhetta's sidebar approach (client, year, location, typology — visible before you read) helps visitors self-sort. SMASH should adapt: client, venue, year, medium tags, tech stack.
- **Challenge-vision-result is dead:** Moment Factory's approach is the weakest. Don't use it.
- **Credits as trust signals:** Pentagram credits the lead partner on every card. SMASH should credit all three roles (Creative Direction: Ash / Production: Veronica / Tech: Emile).
- **Impact metrics matter:** Even without Roosegaarde's environmental data, SMASH can quantify: audience numbers, dwell time, event attendance.
- **Video is non-negotiable for immersive work.** Stills of projection mapping can look flat. Short, looping, ambient video — not a showreel you click play on — should be embedded in every case study.

### Homepage and first impression
- Every strong studio leads with work, not words. This is confirmed 8/8.
- But SMASH needs one additional element: a sharp positioning line for visitors who don't already know the studio. Pentagram can skip this; SMASH can't.
- Working direction: strongest project imagery → one-sentence positioning → curated project grid showing range.
- &Walsh's approach of curating aggressively (show the best 4-6, not everything) is right for the homepage. Full portfolio can live on a dedicated page.

### Tone and language
- **The landscape has two poles, both wrong for SMASH:**
  - Corporate-impersonal (Moment Factory)
  - Art-world-inaccessible (Random International, teamLab, Universal Everything, Roosegaarde)
- **The gap is a practitioner voice** — technically fluent, emotionally warm, direct, confident without being grandiose. &Walsh comes closest but it's one-voice branding, not collaborative.
- **Specific devices that work:** Roosegaarde's provocation questions ("What if..."). &Walsh's declarative, hype-free descriptions. Pentagram's third-person editorial authority.
- **For SMASH, the register should feel like:** explaining the project to a creative director you respect — not up (to impress), not down (to simplify), straight across.

### Trust signals for a studio at SMASH's scale
Large studios use scale, client names, and venue prestige. SMASH needs different levers:
- **Named humans with clear roles** — the three-partner structure is inherently trust-building
- **Technical specificity** — "Built in Unreal Engine 5 with custom TouchDesigner pipeline" is a trust sentence that no art-world studio would write
- **Process visibility** — showing how you work (even briefly) builds trust that a black box never can
- **Outcome data** — audience numbers, event context, repeat clients
- **Quality of documentation** — the photography and video quality IS the trust signal
- **Clear invitation to collaborate** — frictionless contact, "let's talk" energy
- **Three voices, not one** — a differentiator vs. every auteur or anonymous model in the landscape

### 9. Swamp Experience

**For SMASH — this is the closest competitor-reference so far:**

## Emerging conclusions (after 10 references)

These feel repeated and meaningful enough to flag:

1. **The open territory for SMASH is "accessible confidence."** Most studios are either corporate-impersonal or art-world-inaccessible. Gentilhomme and Swamp come closest to the right register — playful, confident, craft-proud — but neither shows the humans behind the work. SMASH can.

2. **The three-partner structure is a genuine differentiator.** Every reference is either anonymous collective (teamLab), auteur-led (Roosegaarde), institutional (Pentagram, Snøhetta), or deliberately faceless network (Gentilhomme). Three named humans with complementary roles is unusual and trust-building. No one does this.

3. **Process visibility is unoccupied territory.** Every reference treats process as a black box. For SMASH, showing the craft (Unreal viewport, TouchDesigner pipeline, projection testing) would be both differentiating and trust-building.

4. **Video is non-negotiable.** Confirmed further by Gentilhomme's video-forward approach. Immersive work loses its essence in stills. Short, ambient, embedded video — not showreels — should be in every case study.

5. **Case studies should be provocation-first, metadata-rich, editorially sequenced.** The best elements from Roosegaarde (provocation), Snøhetta (metadata), &Walsh (editorial rhythm), Pentagram (credits), and Gentilhomme (video overlay, bold categories) can be synthesized into a SMASH-specific format.

6. **The positioning statement needs to be sharper than anything seen in the landscape.** Gentilhomme's "boutique studio that goes big" is the best positioning line seen so far — it owns a specific tension. SMASH needs something equally sharp and specific.

7. **Named work categories beat generic tags.** Swamp's "Swamp Originals" and Gentilhomme's "Airport Mode" / "Immersive Architecture" / "Plug & Play Creations" are memorable and distinctive. SMASH's taxonomy should follow suit — "SMASH Originals" plus categories with personality, not just "Brand Experience" or "Installation."

8. **Punchy copy rhythm works for this space.** Gentilhomme's "Every. Tiny. Detail." and "Consistently. Deliver. Ambitious. Work." demonstrate that rhythmic, declarative copy cuts through better than either corporate smooth or art-world contemplative. This aligns with the practitioner voice hypothesis (H3).
