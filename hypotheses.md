# hypotheses.md

## Purpose

Working hypotheses for the SMASH website. These should be specific enough to guide exploration and risky enough to be proven wrong. If a hypothesis just confirms what we already believe, it's not doing its job.

---

## Positioning hypotheses

### H1 — Transformation framing vs. service categories
SMASH is better positioned through the idea of transforming spaces than through a conventional service-category introduction (installation, projection mapping, etc.).

Why this might be true:
- transformation is emotionally specific and central to the work
- it differentiates from studios that lead with technical capability
- it speaks to what the audience *experiences*, not what the studio *does*

What might challenge this:
- commissioners may need service categories to quickly assess fit ("do they do projection mapping?")
- "transformation" could feel abstract without immediate examples
- Google/search discoverability may depend on concrete service terms

How to test:
- Reference analysis: do studios with concept-led positioning convert as well as capability-led ones?
- Ask Ash/Veronica: how do clients actually describe what they're looking for when they reach out?

Status: **Partially supported (Batch 1)**
Every studio in the direct landscape uses concept/experience framing over service categories. But notably, Moment Factory also uses clear capability taxonomy (Permanent Installations, Live Shows, Destinations) that helps visitors self-sort — and this works. The answer may be "both" — lead with transformation, but make categories findable. The SEO/discoverability concern remains untested and is real.

---

### H2 — Project-led vs. capability-led structure
The work itself (shown through curated case studies) can communicate what SMASH does more effectively than a capability overview.

Why this might be true:
- experiential work is hard to explain abstractly — showing is better
- strong case studies prove both vision and execution simultaneously
- this avoids the "service list" trap

What might challenge this:
- if the portfolio content isn't strong enough (text, imagery, narrative) to carry this weight alone
- if visitors need a capability frame *before* looking at projects to know they're in the right place
- the Codex API is currently empty — no portfolio content exists yet to build on

How to test:
- Inventory what project material actually exists (imagery, video, narrative, credits)
- Test with reference studios: where does project-led work and where does it fail?

Status: **Supported with major caveat (Batch 1)**
Every strong studio in the landscape leads with projects. The work IS the communication. But the caveat is critical: this only works when the project material is strong enough — excellent photography, video, and narrative. Resonance currently has good body text but no imagery, no video, no credits, no outcome data. H2 depends entirely on whether the portfolio material exists or can be created. Without it, a capability frame becomes necessary as a fallback.

### H3 — Warm editorial tone vs. polished agency or art-world register
The right voice for SMASH is warm, specific, and editorial — not corporate-polished and not art-theoretical.

Why this might be true:
- the work is public-facing and accessible — the voice should match
- editorial warmth builds trust faster than polish or abstraction
- it reflects how the team actually talks about their work

What might challenge this:
- premium clients may expect more formal or elevated language
- warmth can drift into casualness, which could undermine credibility
- different sections may need different registers (project pages vs. about vs. CTA)

How to test:
- Draft the same project intro in three registers and compare
- Review reference language that felt right and identify what register it actually uses

Status: **Strongly supported (Batch 1)**
The landscape clusters around two poles: corporate-impersonal (Moment Factory) and art-world-inaccessible (Random International, teamLab, Roosegaarde). Neither serves SMASH's purpose of converting visitors into collaborators. The gap — a warm, direct, practitioner voice — is genuinely open territory. No studio in the batch occupies it. Still needs testing through actual copy drafts, but the competitive case for this register is strong.

## Audience hypotheses

### H4 — Commissioners decide on feeling before capability
Potential clients form a gut impression of fit ("these people get it") before evaluating specific capabilities. The website's first job is emotional resonance, not information delivery.

Why this might be true:
- experiential work is bought on trust and creative alignment
- the best commissions come through relationships and reputation, not RFP filtering
- a strong feeling of quality may matter more than a comprehensive service list

What might challenge this:
- institutional buyers (museums, cities) may have procurement processes that need capability evidence early
- budget holders who aren't the creative leads may need practical information upfront
- some visitors are doing due diligence, not discovery — they already know SMASH and need specifics

How to test:
- Ask Ash/Veronica: what's the actual path from "first heard about SMASH" to "sent an email"?
- Analyze what information past clients needed before committing

Status: Untested

---

### H5 — The site's real competition isn't other studios — it's the reel
Most commissioners will have seen SMASH's work in person or via a showreel before visiting the website. The site isn't the first impression — it's the second. Its job is to confirm and deepen, not to discover.

Why this might be true:
- experiential studios get work through reputation, referrals, and live experience
- the website may function more as validation than attraction
- this would change what needs to be on the homepage entirely

What might challenge this:
- the website may be the first touchpoint for some audiences (press, partners, cold inquiries)
- if SMASH wants to grow into new markets, the site needs to work as a standalone introduction
- this hypothesis is comfortable because it lowers the bar — which makes it worth interrogating

How to test:
- Ask the team: where do most inquiries actually come from? What did people see before they emailed?
- Check analytics from the current site or Smash OS if available

Status: Untested

## Content & structure hypotheses

### H6 — Fewer projects, told deeply, beats a comprehensive portfolio
5–7 deeply told projects will serve SMASH better than showing everything.

Why this might be true:
- depth builds confidence more than breadth
- curation signals premium positioning
- the spatial/experiential nature of the work needs room to breathe

What might challenge this:
- a narrow selection may undersell range and versatility
- different audiences look for different project types — too few means some won't see themselves
- "deeply told" requires strong narrative writing and imagery that may not exist yet for all projects

How to test:
- Inventory available project material — which projects have enough depth?
- Reference analysis: where does curation feel premium and where does it feel thin?

Status: Untested

---

### H7 — The website needs to work harder than SMASH thinks
Studios often underestimate how much clarity a website needs because they understand their own work intuitively. What's obvious to SMASH isn't obvious to a visitor.

Why this might be true:
- the curse of knowledge is real — "we transform spaces" is clear to the team, potentially vague to a stranger
- experiential studios often build beautiful sites that fail to communicate what they actually do
- a visitor should be able to explain SMASH to someone else after 30 seconds on the site

What might challenge this:
- over-explaining can flatten the mystery and ambition of the work
- the right audience may already have enough context from the industry
- being too explicit could make SMASH feel less premium

How to test:
- Show early messaging to someone outside the studio's circle and ask "what do these people do?"
- Review competitor sites: can you tell what they do in 10 seconds?

Status: **Strongly supported (Batch 1)**
Tested the 30-second test across all four references. Only Moment Factory passes it cleanly — and they use scale and client names to do it. teamLab, Random International, and Roosegaarde all require industry context to understand fully. Every one of them could communicate faster with less art-world distance. SMASH, with less brand recognition than any of them, needs to be the clearest of the bunch. A visitor should be able to explain SMASH to someone else in one sentence after 30 seconds on the site.
